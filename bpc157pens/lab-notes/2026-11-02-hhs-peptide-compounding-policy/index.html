HHS peptide compounding policy

INDUSTRY UPDATE

October 26 to November 2 came down to one federal notice. HHS published a draft policy on compounding six peptides. It is not an approval. It does not cover research-use-only products.

Must-know

1) HHS interim policy for six peptides (filed October 28; Federal Register October 30, 91 FR 68526). HHS said it does not plan to take enforcement action against a state-licensed pharmacy, a federal facility, or a licensed physician that compounds a drug with a peptide on the policy list. The six peptides are BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500. Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. signed the notice. Comments are due November 13, 2026.

The pause only applies when the conditions in the policy are met. The product has to match the form and the route the policy allows. Examples: BPC-157 acetate by injection, by mouth, or on the skin. TB-500 acetate and MOTS-c acetate by injection only. Semax free base in the nose only. KPV acetate on the skin or by mouth only. Epitalon acetate by injection, in short courses. The bulk substance has to come from an FDA-registered manufacturer that follows CGMP. The certificate of analysis for that lot has to cover identity, purity, potency, and the absence of endotoxin, heavy metals, microbes, and impurities. One active ingredient per product. A prescription for one patient. The label has to say the product is a compounded drug and is not for resale. Serious side effects and quality problems have to be reported to FDA MedWatch and the state. If a syringe is included, it has to be an FDA-cleared device. A form taken by mouth needs evidence that people absorb that specific formula.

This policy is a draft. It is not binding, and it is not FDA approval. It does not add these peptides to the 503A bulks list. It does not cover 503B outsourcing facilities or research-use-only sales. HHS can withdraw it if a safety or quality problem shows up. A state board can still restrict the practice. The notice is meant to move patients toward controlled U.S. compounding and away from foreign research-use-only material.

PRODUCT SPOTLIGHT

NAD+

NAD+ is the oxidized form of a coenzyme called nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. Cells use it to move electrons while they make energy. Cells also use it up when enzymes repair DNA and help decide which genes stay active. This pen holds 1600 mg of NAD+ in 3 mL, about 533 mg per mL. It is already in solution for laboratory work. No mixing step is required.

Most published human studies that try to raise NAD+ use a precursor, such as nicotinamide riboside or nicotinamide mononucleotide, or a small pilot infusion of NAD+ itself. Researchers study NAD+ to watch redox balance, mitochondrial energy metabolism, and what changes when NAD+ runs low in stress and aging models. It is for research use only.

What researchers look at

  • How cells move electrons in redox reactions
  • Mitochondrial energy metabolism
  • Enzymes that use up NAD+, including sirtuins and PARPs
  • How cells recycle NAD+ through the salvage pathway
  • How direct NAD+ compares with precursors such as NR and NMN

ALSO ON THE RADAR

JAMA critique · orthopaedic review · FDA letters

A JAMA viewpoint published online October 26 critiques the July 2026 Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee votes. Those votes recommended six of seven peptides even though FDA staff had objected. Authors tied to the Center for Science in the Public Interest say following the votes could expand a market of unproven peptides and weaken the advisory-committee process. The piece came out the same week as the HHS policy.

A review in the Journal of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, posted online October 27, covers BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4, the protein TB-500 is taken from. The human evidence is thin: mostly small-animal studies, plus one small uncontrolled case series for BPC-157. The authors say none of these peptides has an FDA-approved muscle or bone use, and they do not recommend routine clinical use. They also flag quality problems in commercial products, including identity, purity, sterility, and consistency from lot to lot.

FDA warning letters to Peak Performance Peptides and Royal Peptides LLC are dated August 24 and showed up on FDA’s site around November 1. FDA treated research-labeled products as unapproved new drugs when the websites claimed a disease treatment or a body effect. The letters name retatrutide, semaglutide, SS-31, tesamorelin, PT-141, and bacteriostatic water. The letters themselves are older than this week. What is new is that they are now public.

Set aside this week: repeat write-ups of the July 2026 advisory-committee meeting, warning-letter roundups from before October 26, clinic sales pages, and older Category 2 commentary. Draft guidances for generic semaglutide and tirzepatide, dated July 28, showed up in search with fresh page dates. They sit outside this seven-day window.

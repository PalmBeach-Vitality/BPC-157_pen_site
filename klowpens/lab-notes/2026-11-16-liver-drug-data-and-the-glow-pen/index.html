Liver-drug data and the GLOW pen

MUST-KNOW

November 9 to November 15 had one result worth knowing. Merck said its experimental drug efinopegdutide met the goals of a mid-stage liver study. The drug acts on GLP-1 and glucagon. It is still in development. It is not approved, and it is not a peptide in the catalog.

The study, MK-6024-013, included about 386 adults with biopsy-confirmed MASH, a form of fatty liver disease with inflammation, plus stage 2 or 3 scarring. People took the drug once a week, at 4, 7, or 10 mg, for 52 weeks.

The liver inflammation cleared, and the scarring did not get worse, in 56 to 63 percent of people on the drug, versus 13 percent on placebo. Scarring improved by at least one stage, without the inflammation getting worse, in 34 to 52 percent versus 21 percent on placebo.

The results were presented at The Liver Meeting and published in The Lancet. Companies are still spending heavily on drugs that hit more than one metabolic target. That attention shows up in conversations about GLP-1-class peptides. Efinopegdutide stays an investigational drug.

PRODUCT SPOTLIGHT

GLOW pen

GLOW is three peptides in one prefilled pen: BPC-157 10 mg, TB-500 10 mg, and GHK-Cu 50 mg, in 3 mL. It is already in solution. There is no mixing step.

Researchers study the three pieces separately. GHK-Cu shows up in skin and tissue-remodeling work. BPC-157 shows up in wound and soft-tissue models. TB-500, a fragment of thymosin beta-4, shows up in repair and cell-migration models. GLOW is the blend name. There is no single landmark human trial of that branded stack. The pen is for laboratory research only.

What researchers look at

  • Skin and matrix remodeling with GHK-Cu
  • Wound and soft-tissue models with BPC-157
  • Cell migration and repair models with TB-500
  • How the three peptides are compared in the same assay
  • Dermal and cosmetic-research characterization

ALSO ON THE RADAR

Teen Zepbound data, telehealth listings, and a knee study

At ObesityWeek, Lilly presented Phase 3 SURMOUNT-ADOLESCENTS results, published at the same time in JAMA. Teenagers with obesity, ages 12 to under 18, took Zepbound (tirzepatide) with lifestyle support. In that group, average BMI fell 21.4 percent at 72 weeks, and more than half no longer met the BMI cutoff for obesity. In a wider group of 160 adolescents, average weight loss was about 40 pounds, and BMI fell 18.5 percent. They also reported better physical comfort and body esteem. The most common side effects were mild to moderate nausea and vomiting, in line with adult studies. Zepbound is an approved prescription drug. This readout does not describe a research-catalog peptide.

Hims and Hers said it will start offering glutathione, sermorelin, and NAD+ in November, and that it plans to add U.S.-made BPC-157 and TB-500 later, under the late-October HHS enforcement-discretion policy. That is a market signal. It is not an FDA approval. The policy still has conditions, and it does not cover research-use-only material.

Retatrutide, an investigational triple agonist, had Phase 3 knee-arthritis results in the New England Journal of Medicine on November 8. Coverage carried into this week. In adults with obesity and painful knee osteoarthritis, pain scores improved by about 1.6 to 1.8 points more than placebo on a 0 to 10 scale, along with substantial weight loss. It is not approved.

No new human study on the catalog peptides this week. Window: Nov 9–15, 2026.
